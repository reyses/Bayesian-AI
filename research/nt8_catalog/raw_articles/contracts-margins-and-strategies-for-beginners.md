# Futures Trading Explained: A Beginner’s Guide to Contracts, Margins, and Strategies

By
NinjaTrader Team

If you’re new to futures trading, understanding the core components is essential before entering the market. Futures offer a unique way to speculate on or hedge against price movements in assets like commodities, indexes, and currencies.

This comprehensive beginner’s guide breaks down three key pillars of futures trading: contracts, margins, and strategies. Whether you're just starting to explore the world of futures or looking to better understand how each piece fits together, this guide can help you establish a strong foundation.

## Contracts: The core of futures trading

At the center of every futures trade is a futures contract—a standardized agreement to buy or sell a specific asset at a predetermined price on a future date. These contracts are traded on regulated exchanges and represent a wide range of underlying assets such as equity indexes, energy, metals, agriculture, and currencies.

### Contract specifications

Each futures contract has unique characteristics:

* The underlying asset (e.g., crude oil, gold, S&P 500 index)
* Contract size (amount of the asset represented)
* Tick size and value (minimum price movement and its value)
* Expiration date (when the contract must be settled)
* Trading hours (when the contract can be traded)

This standardization helps create deep liquidity and ensures that contracts are easily tradable between market participants.

## Margins: Understanding capital requirements

Unlike buying shares of stock, trading futures doesn't require the full contract value upfront. Instead, traders use margin—a good faith deposit to cover losses—to open and maintain positions. This introduces the concept of leverage, which can magnify both profits and losses.

### What is margin in futures trading?

Futures margin consists of two components:

* **Initial margin:** The upfront amount required to open a trade
* **Maintenance margin:** The minimum amount of equity that must be maintained to keep the position open

If your account balance falls below the maintenance margin, your broker may issue a margin call, requiring additional funds or position closure. This system helps protect both traders and brokers from excessive risk, especially in fast-moving markets.

Dive deeper here: [Using Margin in Futures Trading](/learn/how-to-trade-futures/using-margin/)

### How does leverage work?

Because of the relatively low margin requirements in futures, traders can control large positions with smaller amounts of capital—this is known as leverage. While leverage increases exposure and the potential for gains, it also heightens the risk of losses, even from small price movements.

A well-leveraged trade should be paired with risk controls and awareness of contract volatility.

For more on leverage, see: [Margin and Leverage in the Futures Markets](/futures/blogs/margin-and-leverage-in-futures-markets/)

### What are the risks of trading on margin with leverage?

Trading on margin with leverage introduces several risks:

* Rapid capital erosion in volatile markets
* Margin calls requiring additional funds
* Amplified losses compared to non-leveraged trading

Understanding and managing these risks is a vital part of any futures trading plan. --

## Strategies: Building a trading approach

Once you understand how contracts and margin work, the next step is to explore futures trading strategies to help guide when to enter and exit trades, how to manage risk, and how to interpret market signals.

But strategy alone isn’t enough—successful trading begins with a well-defined trading plan that outlines your objectives, risk tolerance, markets of interest, and the specific rules you’ll follow for entering and exiting trades. By committing to a structured approach, you can remove emotional decision-making and stay consistent, even in volatile conditions.

Here are a few foundational strategies beginners can consider as part of a broader trading plan:

#### Trend-following

This strategy involves identifying a market direction—either upward or downward—and trading in that direction. Traders often use technical indicators like moving averages or the MACD (moving average convergence divergence) to help confirm the presence and strength of a trend. Trend-following can help you reduce overtrading and align trades with broader market momentum.

#### Range trading

When markets are not trending, they often move within a defined range between support and resistance levels. Range traders seek to capitalize on these horizontal movements by buying near support and selling near resistance. Incorporating this strategy into your plan requires careful monitoring of price action and confirmation indicators to avoid false signals.

#### Breakout trading

Breakout traders focus on key price levels where a market has previously stalled. Once the price breaks through these levels—often accompanied by increased volume or volatility—it may indicate the beginning of a new trend. A breakout strategy benefits from having clearly defined entry and exit rules to avoid reacting impulsively to false breakouts.

#### News or event-driven trading

Economic announcements, earnings reports, and geopolitical events can all create short-term volatility in the futures markets. Traders using a news-based approach must stay informed and act quickly around scheduled events. While potentially profitable, this strategy also introduces greater risk and should be used with tight risk management protocols.

Incorporating one or more of these strategies into a formal trading plan can help you stay disciplined and aligned with your overall goals. A solid plan not only defines how you’ll trade—but also why, when, and under what conditions.
